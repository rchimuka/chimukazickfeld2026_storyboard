What happens to climate and carbon stocks when CO₂ levels decline?

The climate-carbon cycle does not simply respond in reverse.

Chimuka & Zickfeld · Biogeosciences · 2026

↓ Scroll to explore

For most of recent history

we have added CO₂ to the atmosphere.

But pathways to limit warming below 1.5 or 2°C below preindustrial levels require

removing CO₂ from the atmosphere.

So, an important question emerges

Can the impact of increasing CO₂ on the climate and carbon stocks be cancelled out by reducing CO₂ by the same amount?

The experiment

Let's choose a climate state to start from — say, 560 ppm of atmospheric CO₂.

Then change atmospheric CO₂ by the same magnitude, symmetrically, in opposite directions.

In the real world, land and ocean carbon respond to both CO₂ and the temperature it causes — so we ran two more experiments to disentangle them.

But why?

As CO₂ rises, the CO₂-fertilization effect on plants saturates — growth slows down. As CO₂ falls, there's no equivalent saturation, so land can lose carbon faster than it gains it.

As CO₂ rises, the ocean's capacity to buffer CO₂ taken up declines; as it falls, buffer capacity increases more — so the ocean also loses carbon faster than it takes it up.

What do we see

Any CO₂ increase is countered by land and ocean uptake. Any CO₂ decrease is countered by land and ocean release.

This is known as the — and it can be measured with a metric called beta (β): the sensitivity of land and ocean carbon stocks to CO₂ change, in billion metric tonnes of carbon (GtC) per ppm.

In plain terms: removing CO₂ from the air leads to more carbon release from the land and ocean than emitting the same amount leads to land and ocean carbon uptake.

The change in CO₂ is identical in size — only the direction flips — yet the feedback is stronger with declining CO₂.

But why?

As temperature rises, plant respiration increases and (NPP) declines, decreasing land carbon stocks. As temperature falls more, less energy is needed for respiration, so NPP rises more, and even more carbon is sequestered by the land.

Ocean circulation strengthens more under cooling than it weakens under warming, which on its own would favor greater carbon loss under cooling than carbon gain under warming, as stronger circulation is more efficient at transferring carbon from the deep ocean to the surface ocean where it is released to the atmosphere.

However, CO₂ solubility in seawater rises more with cooling than it falls with warming, favouring more carbon uptake with cooling. This effect dominates, and overall, the ocean loses more carbon with warming than it sequesters with cooling.

What do we see

Any warming is amplified by carbon loss from land and ocean. Any cooling is amplified by carbon uptake into land and ocean.

This is known as the — measured with a metric called gamma (γ): the sensitivity of land and ocean carbon stocks to temperature change, in GtC per °C.

In plain terms: land and ocean carbon stocks react more strongly to a degree of warming than to a degree of cooling.

Here the temperature changes themselves were asymmetric (+1°C vs −1.5°C), which partly explains why these metrics differ too — the feedback is stronger with warming.

What TCRE tells us

For a given temperature limit, TCRE gives the maximum total emissions allowed (the total carbon budget). Subtract what's already been emitted, and you get the remaining carbon budget. Three metrics can be used to calculate the TCRE:

a

Feedback metrics

Beta (β) and gamma (γ) — the numbers from the last two chapters.

b

Climate sensitivity

How much temperature change results per unit of CO₂ increase or decrease, under each direction.

c

& fraction

How much of the CO₂ we emit stays in the atmosphere, and how much of the CO₂ we remove stays out of it.

This metric shapes real climate policy.

TCRE-derived carbon budgets are already used to set emissions targets worldwide.

Why this matters

Reliance on feedback metrics derived only from positive-emission scenarios can misrepresent how the Earth system actually responds under net-negative CO₂ emissions.

These misrepresentations can propagate into TCRE itself, resulting in inaccurate carbon budget estimations.

Limitations: we recommend that this experiment be repeated in multi-model analyses, particularly with models including physical climate feedbacks and explicit nitrogen–land coupling.

Glossary

BGC mode
A version of the model where the temperature response is turned off, so land and ocean only feel the CO₂ change, not the warming or cooling it causes.
RAD mode
A version of the model where the CO₂ change itself is turned off, so land and ocean only feel the resulting temperature change.
Concentration-carbon feedback (β)
How strongly land and ocean carbon stocks respond to a change in atmospheric CO₂ concentration alone.
Climate-carbon feedback (γ)
How strongly land and ocean carbon stocks respond to a change in global temperature alone.
NPP — Net Primary Production
The net rate at which plants build new biomass, after accounting for their own respiration.
AMOC
The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation — a major ocean current system that moves heat and carbon between the surface and deep ocean.
TCRE
Transient Climate Response to cumulative carbon Emissions — degrees of warming per trillion tonnes of carbon emitted. Under negative emissions, this study calls it TCRR (the "R" for removed).
Airborne fraction
The share of emitted CO₂ that stays in the atmosphere rather than being absorbed by land or ocean.
Removal fraction
The share of removed CO₂ that stays out of the atmosphere rather than being replaced by land or ocean release.
